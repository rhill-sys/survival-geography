      How this is scored

      Every state gets a 0–10 rating on seven measures: isolation (how controllable its borders and approaches are), food self-sufficiency (calories produced against calories eaten, without imported inputs), energy independence (domestic generation that survives a fuel cutoff — hydro, geothermal, nuclear — plus refining capacity), state capacity, low strategic value (10 means nobody's target list includes you), dispersal, and climate survivability without industrial inputs. Each lens weights those seven differently. The nuclear lens adds a bonus for the southern hemisphere, where both fallout and the agricultural effects of nuclear winter fall considerably lighter.

      The weightings are mine and they are arguable — that's the point of exposing them. The bars in the assessment panel show which measures the active lens is actually leaning on, so you can see what's driving any given score rather than taking the number on faith.

      What this map cannot tell you. It scores geography, and geography is the second-most important variable. The best-documented predictor of who comes through a collapse is whether you are embedded in a community that cooperates under stress — which is a function of how long you've lived somewhere and who knows your name, not of hydroelectric capacity. A person who relocates to a top-decile square as a stranger is very plausibly worse off than one who stays put in a mid-tier square among twenty years of relationships. And in the scenario most people mean by "AI apocalypse" — a capable, misaligned system that wants humans gone — none of this matters at all. That scenario has no geography. These four do.
